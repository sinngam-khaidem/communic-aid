Communic-Aid · design plan

How Communic-Aid senses

An iPhone below the monitor watches the face. The Mac turns what it sees into a point on the screen and four confirmation signals: blink and hold, raised eyebrows, "yes" and the switch.

From the phone to the Mac

On the phone

The phone runs Apple's face tracking on the front camera, about 60 times a second. For each frame it works out where the eyes are and which way they look, then writes both in the phone's own coordinates. The phone sits still below the monitor, so its coordinates are a fixed reference for the screen. Each frame goes to the Mac as one small message:

FieldWhat it is
eyeThe point between the two eyes, in metres from the camera
dirThe direction the eyes are looking, as an arrow of length 1
headThe direction the nose points, as an arrow of length 1. Used only for head pointing.
blinkL, blinkRHow closed each eye is, from 0 (open) to 1 (shut)
browIn, browOutL, browOutRHow raised the inner brows and each outer brow are, from 0 to 1
trackedWhether a face is visible. When it is lost, the phone still sends a frame saying so.

Speech is handled on the phone too. When it hears "yes", it sends a separate "yes" message. The sound itself never leaves the phone.

On the Mac

The receiver listens on port 5005 and passes each message on: frames go to gaze and signals, "yes" goes to signals. It uses UDP, which never waits or resends, so a lost packet is simply skipped. At 60 frames a second, nobody notices one missing frame.

The receiver also notices when the phone goes quiet. If no packet arrives for 2 seconds, the screen says the phone has stopped sending, so a changed Wi-Fi address or a sleeping phone is easy to spot.

Turning gaze into a point on the screen

1. Find where the gaze meets the phone's plane

Picture a flat sheet standing upright through the phone's camera. The monitor sits just above the phone, in nearly the same sheet. Starting at the eyes, follow the gaze direction until it hits that sheet. That hit point, in millimetres from the camera, is where the person is looking.

steps = -eye.z ÷ dir.z hit = eye + steps × dir (keep x and y, in mm)

For the head only study mode (--pointing head), the same steps use where the nose points instead of where the eyes look. The cursor then follows the head and ignores the eyes. It needs its own calibration.

An older phone build that does not send eye falls back to two angles: how far the gaze turns left or right, and up or down. That works, but moving the head shifts the point.

2. Calibrate: learn how hit points line up with pixels

The hit point is in millimetres on the phone's plane, but the screen needs pixels. The phone is not exactly in the monitor's plane, and the face tracker has its own small biases. So instead of measuring the setup, the system learns the link from the person:

  • A dot appears at 9 spots: a 3 by 3 grid at 10%, 50% and 90% of the width and height.
  • At each dot it waits 1.2 seconds for the eyes to settle, then collects hit points for 1 second. Frames with closed eyes are skipped.
  • It keeps the median of those points, so one stray glance or blink cannot pull the result.
  • If the phone pauses or the face is lost during a dot, that dot starts over instead of being skipped.

3. Fit a gentle curve from millimetres to pixels

With 9 pairs of "hit point, dot position", it fits one small formula for the screen's x and another for its y. Each uses the two hit numbers u and v (scaled to a similar size first), their product and their squares:

screen x = a0 + a1·u + a2·v + a3·u·v + a4·u² + a5·v² screen y = b0 + b1·u + b2·v + b3·u·v + b4·u² + b5·v²

The 12 numbers a and b are chosen by least squares: the ones that put the predicted points closest to the 9 dots. A tiny extra term keeps them from growing wild when the data is thin. The squared terms let the mapping bend a little, which absorbs a tilted phone, lens distortion and a mirrored axis. This kind of second order mapping is a common choice in eye trackers (Cerrolaza and others, 2012).

Two checks come out of calibration:

  • Error: the average distance between where it now predicts each dot and the dot itself. Over 100 px, it asks to calibrate again.
  • Spread: how much the gaze wobbles while holding still on a dot. This is the person's steady baseline. Later, gaze that wobbles 1.5 times more than this counts as shaky.

It needs at least 6 good dots to fit. Fewer than that, and calibration fails rather than guessing.

4. Every frame after that

  • No face: the gaze is marked as missing until the face is back.
  • Eyes closed: the point is frozen where it was. The tracker's gaze swings as the eyelids come down, so without this a blink would drag the cursor. It also means a blink and hold confirms the button that was being looked at.
  • Smoothing: each new point moves the cursor 30% of the way toward it: shown = 0.3 × new + 0.7 × shown. This calms the jitter while still following real eye movements quickly.
  • Drift correction: after each selection that worked, it compares where the gaze was in the 300 ms before firing with the centre of that button. It then moves a small correction 10% of the way to close the gap. The correction never grows past 80 px, so one odd selection cannot drag the cursor away. This uses the idea that people look at what they select (Hornof and Halverson, 2002).
  • Moving the window: calibration is learned in pixels of the whole screen, not of the page. The page reports where its top left corner sits on the screen, checked every second. Leaving full screen or moving the window therefore does not shift the gaze.

The four signals

A signal is the second step on a risky button. The button fills with gaze, then waits up to 4 seconds for the signal it asked for. The right signal fires it. A different signal is shown as the wrong one, and the button keeps waiting. Looking away for half a second, or running out of time, cancels it.

Blink and hold

both eyes closed for 500 ms

Both eyes must be more closed than the person's closed level. A natural blink lasts well under half a second, and a wink only closes one eye, so neither fires.

Raised eyebrows

brows up for 300 ms, eyes open

The brow level is the average of the three brow values. It must be above the person's raised level while the eyes are open, so one movement never counts as two signals.

"Yes"

the word "yes", once per 1.5 s

Apple's speech recognition runs on the phone, with Indian English when the phone has it. If the phone cannot recognise speech on its own, "yes" stays off instead of sending audio to a server. A second "yes" within 1.5 seconds is ignored, so one word is never counted twice.

Switch

Space, Enter or S

An accessibility switch that types a key. Each press is a signal at once. Three presses within 2 seconds is the way out: it goes back to the chat list from anywhere and keeps the draft.

Holding, and trying

Blink and eyebrows use the same hold rule. The clock starts when the value crosses the person's level. The signal fires once, when the hold is long enough. If the person lets go after at least 150 ms but before the hold is reached, it is noted as an attempt. Attempts tell the learner "they tried but could not finish", which is different from "they never meant to press this".

Fitting the levels to each person: the signal check

Faces differ. One person's relaxed eyes already read 0.2; another's brows barely move. So before use, a short check sets each person's own levels:

  • Relax, looking at the middle for 2 seconds. This records how open the eyes and how high the brows are at rest.
  • Relax, looking at a dot at the top for 2 seconds. Looking up lifts the brows on its own, so the higher of the two brow readings becomes the rest level. Looking up should never count as raised eyebrows.
  • Close both eyes until a tone, 3 times. Raise the eyebrows, 3 times. Say "yes", 3 times. Press the switch, 3 times. Each try has 6 seconds, and only counts if the face was relaxed first.

From the tries it sets:

  • Closed level: halfway between the relaxed eyes and the average of the closed tries.
  • Raised level: halfway between the brows at rest and the average of the raised tries.
  • Which signals this person can use: those that worked in at least 2 of 3 tries. The others are never asked for.

Until someone does the check, both levels start at 0.5.
