Communic-Aid · design plan, section 6

How Communic-Aid learns

Two small learners watch what each person does and adjust to them. Nobody is ever asked to rate anything: everything is read from behaviour.

The dwell learner sets how long a person has to look at an everyday button (tiers 0 and 1: navigation and typing). The signal chooser decides, for risky buttons (tiers 2 and 3: sending, calling, deleting), whether to ask for a second signal and which one: blink and hold, raised eyebrows, saying "yes", or the switch.

1Reading what happened

Every look and every request for a signal ends in one of four outcomes. An outcome is only decided once its time window has closed, so the learner never learns from a guess.

A look at an everyday button

OutcomeHow the app knows
workedThe button fired and was not undone within 5 s. For a letter, the next key was not Backspace within 3 s.
accidentIt fired, then was undone within 5 s, or a letter was followed straight away by Backspace.
gave upThe person looked away before the fill finished, then came back to the same button within 2 s. They meant it, but the wait felt too long.
passed byThey looked away and did not come back. A stray glance; the app notes how long it lasted.

A request for a second signal

OutcomeHow the app knows
workedThe right signal came in time and the action was not undone.
accidentThe signal came, then the action was undone within 5 s.
gave upIt timed out, but the person tried: a wrong signal, a half blink, a late signal, or a look back within 3 s.
blocked an accidentIt timed out with no attempt and no return. The look was probably a mistake, and the signal stopped it.

Undo, then Redo, counts as worked: the undo was the mistake, not the action.

2The situation

The same person needs different settings when tired or when their gaze is shaky. Two yes or no questions sort every moment into one of four groups. Each learner keeps a separate set of numbers for each group, so what it learns on a tired evening never overrides a fresh morning.

  • Tired means 20 minutes into the session, after 6 pm, or 2 or more accidents or give-ups in the last 6 tries.
  • Shaky means gaze wobbles more than 1.5 times as much as it did during calibration.
  • The dwell learner keeps six counts per group (section 3). The signal chooser keeps a tally for each option, in each group and tier (section 4).
  • A group with little data of its own borrows the totals of all four groups: the dwell learner until the group has 30 looks, the signal chooser until it has 20 tries. A new situation never starts from nothing.

3The dwell learner

It decides how long a person has to look at an everyday button (tiers 0 and 1). To do that it learns two habits of the person:

  • Stray glances: how long do looks that were not meant usually last? If they are short, a shorter dwell is safe.
  • Patience: how long will the person keep looking at a button they want before giving up? If not long, a long dwell causes give-ups.

What it counts

For each of the four groups it keeps these six numbers. They start at small guesses so the first choices are sensible, and every outcome from section 1 adds to them.

#CountStarts atGrows when
1Looks that were meant2+1 for each worked or gave up
2Looks that were not meant2+1 for each accident or passed by
3Stray glances seen3+1 for each passed by
4Total stray glance time900 ms+ the glance's length for each passed by; + the dwell for each accident (that glance lasted at least the dwell)
5Give-ups seen1+1 for each gave up
6Total waiting time5000 ms+ how long they looked before each gave up; + the dwell for each worked (they waited at least that long)

What it works out from the counts

share of looks meant = #1 / (#1 + #2) typical stray glance = #4 / #3 (average length of a stray glance) typical patience = #6 / #5 (average wait before giving up)

Example: one morning

A person in the rested + steady group makes 40 selections that work, undoes 2, gives up 6 times, and their eyes pass over buttons 110 times by accident, for about a quarter of a second each. Adding those to the starting values:

CountValueWorked outValue
#1 meant, #2 not meant48, 114share of looks meant0.30
#3 stray glances, #4 their total time113, 32,565 mstypical stray glance288 ms
#5 give-ups, #6 total waiting time7, 71,300 mstypical patience10.2 s

Stray glances are short and the person is patient, so a shorter dwell is safe. The learner's pick is 800 ms for tier 0 (navigation) and 950 ms for tier 1 (typing), which costs more when it goes wrong. The dwell then moves towards these picks slowly, as described below.

How it picks a dwell

It tries every dwell d from 800 to 2000 ms and gives each a score, using the three numbers above:

chance a stray glance lasts d or longer A(d) = e^(-d / typical stray glance) chance a meant look gives up before d G(d) = 1 - e^(-d / typical patience) score(d) = share meant × [ (1 - G) × (1 - 0.6 × d in seconds) - 0.5 × G ] - share not meant × A × accident cost of the tier

In words: a look that worked is worth 1, minus 0.6 for each second waited. A give-up costs 0.5. An accident costs the tier's accident cost. The dwell with the best score is its pick.

One detail keeps it learning: instead of using the three numbers exactly, it uses values drawn at random close to them, wider apart when it has seen little (this is called Thompson sampling). With only the 3 starting stray glances, the typical stray glance could be drawn anywhere from about 140 to 1090 ms. After 113 glances, it stays between about 250 and 340 ms. So it tries different dwells while unsure and settles as it learns.

Staying calm

A dwell that jumps around feels random and stressful, so it moves slowly and only after real selections.

  • The first 20 selections in a group stay at 1500 ms. Stray glances never count towards this.
  • After that, each selection moves the dwell 5% towards the learner's pick, but at most 10 ms shorter or up to 100 ms longer. Accidents are fixed quickly; speed comes slowly.
  • It never goes below 800 ms or above 2000 ms.

Risky buttons (tiers 2 and 3) keep a fixed 1000 ms fill before asking for a signal. The undo strip is fixed at 800 ms.

4The signal chooser

For Send, React and End call (tier 2) it can choose dwell only or a signal. For Call, Delete and Forward (tier 3) a signal is always required, and it only chooses which.

First, rule out what is not allowed

  • Signals the person switched off in Settings, or that failed their signal check.
  • Blink and eyebrows while tracking is poor (the camera cannot see them reliably).
  • Dwell only on tier 3.

What it counts

To remember how each option has gone, the chooser keeps a tally. A tally is four counts, one for each outcome in section 1: how many times it worked, how many times the person gave up, how many accidents it blocked, and how many accidents got through.

There is not one tally per option. Each tally belongs to one group (section 2), one tier and one option. "Blink, tier 3, tired + shaky" has its own tally, and "blink, tier 3, rested + steady" has another. That is how it can learn that blinking works in the morning but fails in the evening.

On tier 2 there are 5 options (dwell only, blink, eyebrows, "yes", switch), and on tier 3 there are 4 (dwell only is not allowed). With 4 groups, that makes at most 4 × (5 + 4) = 36 tallies. A tally is only created the first time an option is used in that situation.

A new tally does not start at zero. It starts with a few counts already in it, so an option nobody has tried yet still gets a fair chance:

Optionworkedgave upblockedaccident
Dwell only (tier 2)1001
Each signal1111
The signal chosen as "Try first" in Settings3111

After each try on a tier 2 or 3 button, it adds 1 to the matching count in the matching tally. For example, if Rekha blinks to confirm Delete in the evening and it works, the "worked" count in her tired, tier 3, blink tally goes up by 1. Nothing else is stored.

How it picks

A tally gives the chance of each outcome: a tally of 9, 2, 2, 1 means worked 64%, gave up 14%, blocked 14%, accident 7%. The score of an option is each chance times what that outcome is worth, added up. Like the dwell learner, it uses chances drawn at random close to the tally (wide when the tally is small), then asks for the option with the best score.

OutcomeWorthExample, tier 3 Call (cost 8)

gave up-0.5-0.5

Effort is 0.35 plus 0.25 for each second the person usually takes to give that signal, so a quick switch press costs less than a slow "yes".

A worked example

Rekha deletes a message in the evening. After about 10 tries of each, her tallies look like this:

Optionworkedgave upblockedaccidentAverage score
Blink and hold3722-1.15
Raise eyebrows9221-0.25

The score for raise eyebrows, using the worths above:

9/14 × 0.35 (worked) + 2/14 × -0.5 (gave up) + 2/14 × 1.2 (blocked an accident) + 1/14 × -8 (accident) = -0.25

Scores are negative here only because an accident on tier 3 costs so much; what matters is which option scores higher. Her blinks often fail when she is tired, so eyebrows win clearly. The random draw still picks blink now and then, so if her blinks get better, the chooser will notice. In the morning she is in a different group with its own tallies, where blink may win.

All of these live in config.toml, and the costs can also be changed from the study panel during a pilot.

Changing a cost never wipes what was learned. The learners store only counts of what happened; costs are applied at the moment of choosing, so a new value takes effect on the very next choice.

6Watching it live

While the core runs, the terminal prints one line per learning event:

CHOOSE call tier 3 tired+steady -> say "yes" scores: say "yes" +0.44 raise eyebrows -0.38 switch -0.82 OUTCOME open priya tier 0 worked reward +0.10 dwell 1500 ms DWELL tired+steady: warm-up 1 of 20 selections, staying at 1500 ms OUTCOME call tier 3 worked reward +0.53 check: say "yes" ROUTER tier 3 tired+steady say "yes": worked 1, gave up 0, blocked an accident 0, accident 0

The same events, with their rewards, are saved in data/<person>/events.jsonl for analysis after the study.
